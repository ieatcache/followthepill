01 / THE CORE ISSUE

One drug. Many prices. A sprawling financial system.

The legitimate functions of drug companies, distributors, wholesalers, PBMs and pharmacies are different. That distinction can also make it difficult for outsiders to know who earned what from a prescription.

The price question

Who sets the list price?

WAC is the manufacturer's published list price to wholesalers or direct purchasers before many discounts. It is not manufacturing cost or actual insurer payment. [9]

The ownership question

Who controls each asset?

FDA approval holders, NDC labelers, manufacturers, wholesalers, and ultimate investors may be different legal entities. Some are affiliated; others merely contract with one another.

The transparency question

Who pockets the margin?

Public sources show parts of the chain, but not all transfer prices, PBM claims, rebates, acquisition costs, or final net revenue by NDC.

“The existence of a complicated corporate network is not, by itself, evidence of fraud. The real question is what the contracts and actual net payments show.”
Crucial separation: Carwin markets Lofena and Carwin-labeled generic diclofenac 25 mg. Rubicon manufactures a separately labeled diclofenac 25 mg product distributed by AdvaGen. Available records establish participation in the same drug market—not that Carwin is owned by Rubicon or that money from Carwin's product flows to Rubicon. [5] [6]

03 / FOLLOW THE ECONOMIC BENEFIT

Five ways value moves through this system.

Not every transaction involves the same company, and no single party necessarily receives all these revenue streams.

01 → PRODUCT SALES

Manufacturer → marketer

Rubicon can develop or manufacture products; AdvaGen and Validus commercialize generic and branded drugs. Sales and service revenue support the corporate group.

02 → DISTRIBUTION

Marketer → wholesalers / labelers

Commercial partners such as TruPharma provide market access, labeling or distribution. Each party's share depends on private contractual terms.

03 → INTERNAL SERVICES

Company → group logistics

Rubicon's related-party filings disclose AdvaGen and Validus purchases from AimRx 3PL, demonstrating a logistics service relationship within the group. [3]

04 → RELATED-PARTY PAYMENTS

Rubicon → MedOne / Otrio

The company disclosed leave-and-license fees paid to MedOne Pharma Labs and purchases of services from Otrio Ventures, entities influenced by key management or their relatives. [3]

05 → ACQUISITIONS & EQUITY

Capital → new assets

Rubicon acquired brands, facilities and an 85% stake in Arinna. It separately disclosed investments agreed with GEn1E and NeuroNasal. Purchases can expand future earnings capacity. [1] [11]

THE BIG PICTURE

Shareholders → residual returns

Once all costs and liabilities are accounted for, shareholders can benefit through retained earnings, valuation increases and any dividends. These are different from fees paid to contracted businesses.

What public records prove—and what they don't.

They prove the existence of subsidiaries, acquisitions, labeled drugs, contractual relationships and disclosed related-party transactions. They do not by themselves prove illegal kickbacks, manipulated reimbursements, personal wrongdoing or an unlawful coordinated pricing arrangement.

Why the 25 mg strength exists

Diclofenac potassium immediate-release tablets were originally developed as a quicker-onset alternative to certain delayed-release diclofenac formulations. FDA records describe a 25 mg product associated with the 1993 Cataflam approval; in 2003, the FDA ruled its withdrawal was not for safety or effectiveness reasons. [12]

What could explain the price?

A niche strength, separate approval and NDC, manufacturer list-price decisions, distribution strategy and payer coverage all potentially matter. None of that establishes which factor drove this particular price or whether its economics are sustainable.

08 / QUESTIONS THAT DESERVE REAL ANSWERS

What would actually expose abuse—or disprove it?

The public evidence supports tough scrutiny. The next step is to examine the transactions, not jump from association to guilt.

01. What is the current WAC for each specific NDC?

Get date-stamped manufacturer/list-price records for the exact package codes, rather than old wholesaler snippets.

02. What did pharmacies truly pay?

Compare invoices, credited discounts, chargebacks, returns and the appropriate period's NADAC.

03. What did insurers and PBMs actually reimburse?

Review adjudicated claims by BIN/PCN/group, quantity, date, and contract—including spread and post-adjudication adjustments.

04. Who owned the ANDA at the transaction date?

Check Orange Book ownership, labeling and supply agreements. A distributor or labeler does not automatically own the approval.

05. Were related-party transactions on reasonable terms?

Compare disclosed fees, contracts, board approvals and market rates for leasing, staffing and logistics services.

06. Are apparent conflicts actually documented?

Trace ultimate beneficial ownership and legal entitlements. Shared customers, investors or trade partners do not prove collusion.

The argument worth making publicly

Prescription drug pricing is difficult for patients and even pharmacies to audit because one NDC can sit inside multiple layers of ownership, regulatory approvals, manufacturing, wholesaling and payer contracts. This investigation shows real connections and a striking catalog price example, but it does not establish illegal conduct. Transparency about the actual net prices and contractual economics is the missing piece.
